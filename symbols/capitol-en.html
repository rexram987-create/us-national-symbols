The United States Capitol

The seat of the Legislative Branch. A massive structure of stone and marble symbolizing the power of the people, historic political compromises, and the stability (and fragility) of the Republic.

1. Historical Identity: The Seat of the Legislature

  • Years of Construction: The cornerstone was laid on September 18, 1793, by George Washington (in a Masonic ceremony). The building evolved in waves: it was burned in 1814, restored and expanded until 1826, and later underwent a monumental expansion (adding the massive dome and new wings) beginning in 1851 and concluding after the Civil War in 1868.
  • Visionaries / Initiators: The Founding Fathers, led by President George Washington and Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson, as part of establishing the new federal city.
  • Main Builders / Architects: The project saw many architects due to its immense scale. William Thornton designed the original plan, but the man who led the construction of the "New Capitol" and its famous dome was Thomas U. Walter.
  • Reason for Creation: The U.S. Constitution (Article I) established a bicameral Congress (House of Representatives and Senate) to represent the people. A colossal federal structure was required to house hundreds of representatives from across the continent, serving as the "Temple of Law" – the place where American democracy is forged.

2. Etymological Breakdown: The Origin of "Capitol"

Capitol vs. Capital

Thomas Jefferson (who was an avid amateur classicist of ancient Rome) suggested the name for the building. There is a fundamental difference in spelling and meaning:

  • Capital (City / Wealth): From the Latin word caput (head). Refers to the primary city of a state (Washington D.C. is the Capital).
  • Capitol (The Building itself): The name comes directly from the **Capitoline Hill** in ancient Rome. In Rome, this hill was the religious and political center of the Roman Republic, home to the Temple of Jupiter.

The Etymological Meaning: The Latin word Capitolium is composed of caput (head) and olium. A Roman legend says that while digging the temple's foundations, an intact human skull (caput) was found, belonging to an ancient warrior named Aulus. Therefore, the Capitol is "The Hill with the Head". Metaphorically, Jefferson and Washington named the building to symbolize that it is the **"Political Head"** and the sacred center of the new American Republic, just as the hill was in Rome.

3. The People Behind the Symbol

William Thornton - The Original Architect

Etymological Breakdown of the Name:
  • William: Derived from the Germanic name "Wilhelm". Composed of the words wil (will or desire) and helm (helmet or protection). Meaning: "resolute protector".
  • Thornton: An Old English toponymic (place-based) name. Composed of thorn and ton (settlement or farm). Meaning: "thorn-tree settlement".

Lifespan: 1759 – 1828.

Childhood & Background: Born on Jost Van Dyke (in the British Virgin Islands) to a Quaker merchant family. He was never formally trained as an architect; he was educated as a physician (studying in Scotland), an inventor, and a polymath who taught himself architecture from sketchbooks. He immigrated to the United States in 1786.

Architectural Ideology: Thornton believed in simplicity, elegance, and a return to the roots of democracy found in Greece and the Roman Republic. His designs reflected the Georgian and Neoclassical styles. He believed the building should be massive enough to project power, but clearly divided (two separate wings - for the Senate and the House) to reflect constitutional balance and the fact that power is not concentrated in the hands of one individual.

Achievements & Role in Creation: When Secretary of State Jefferson and President Washington announced a design competition for the Capitol, they were disappointed by the submissions. Thornton heard of this and submitted a late, self-taught plan featuring a Roman temple-like center and two rectangular wings. Washington was thrilled, declaring it exhibited "grandeur, simplicity, and beauty". Thornton won the $500 prize and a city lot. Although his original design underwent many changes, his basic plan for its location and proportions determined the familiar facade of the Capitol we know today.

Thomas U. Walter - Designer of the Great Dome

Etymological Breakdown of the Name:
  • Thomas: Originates from the Aramaic name "Te'oma", meaning "Twin".
  • Ustick (Middle Name): A Cornish name (from Cornwall, England), likely derived from an ancient portmanteau related to a specific place.
  • Walter: An ancient Germanic name (Walthari). Composed of wald (rule or power) and hari (army or warrior). Meaning: "commander of the army".

Lifespan: 1804 – 1887.

Childhood & Background: Born in Philadelphia to a family of builders, learning the construction trade from his father. He studied architectural drawing and fine arts, apprenticing under renowned architects. Walter became one of the founders of the American Institute of Architects (AIA).

Architectural Ideology: Walter represented the American "Greek Revival" and Neo-Renaissance generation. As the US grew from 13 states to 31 states (by the mid-19th century), the original wings were no longer sufficient. Walter believed the building had to grow not only in width but to demonstrate immense vertical dominance. He embraced engineering innovation during the Industrial Revolution: using **Cast Iron** as a raw material to create monumental structures the likes of which had never been seen on the American continent.

Achievements & Role in Creation: Walter was appointed "Architect of the Capitol" in 1851. He added the two massive contemporary wings (Senate and House), which dwarfed the building's old, original wood and copper dome. Consequently, Walter designed the Capitol's **colossal cast-iron dome** – an incredible engineering feat weighing nearly 9,000,000 pounds (approx. 4,000 metric tons). Construction of the dome continued even during the Civil War, by order of President Abraham Lincoln, to show the people that "the Union continues".

4. Anatomy, Engineering, & The Laborers

Sub-section 1: Parts of the Constitutional Complex

The building is meticulously divided to reflect the constitutional structure of the United States – two legislative houses, united beneath a single Rotunda in the center:

A. The Rotunda & Dome

The central heart connecting the two houses. This is not a platform for legislation, but a ceremonial area designated for national events, such as lying in state for presidents and distinguished citizens.

  • The Dome: Designed by Thomas Walter, made of 8,909,200 pounds of cast iron. Although it looks like stone from the outside, it is a double-shelled iron structure.
  • The Statue of Freedom: Placed atop the dome. A massive bronze statue (19.5 feet tall, weighing 15,000 pounds) of a female figure wearing a military helmet adorned with eagle feathers. (Important not to confuse with the Statue of Liberty in New York).
  • The Ceiling Fresco ("The Apotheosis of Washington"): A massive mural inside the dome, painted by artist Constantino Brumidi, depicting George Washington ascending to the heavens surrounded by Roman gods.
B. The South Wing (House of Representatives)

The wing added in the mid-19th century, serving as the lower house representing the people.

  • The Chamber: The largest room in the Capitol. Here sit the 435 members of Congress, represented according to their state's population size. This is also the chamber where the President delivers the annual "State of the Union" address.
C. The North Wing (The Senate)

The upper house. A more prestigious, quiet, and smaller chamber.

  • The Chamber: Contains individual wooden desks for 100 Senators (two from each state, regardless of size, to maintain republican equality). Each Senator has an inherited desk, usually with the names of previous Senators who used it carved into the drawer.

Sub-section 2: Technical Specifications (Behind the Scenes)

The building covers an enormous footprint (approximately 1.5 million square feet, or 139,000 square meters) and is one of the most complex structures in America:

  • Rooms: ~600
  • Windows: 658
  • Elevators: ~80
  • Dome Height: 288 feet (88m)
  • Stairs: 365 (to the dome top)
  • Statues: 100+ (National Statuary Hall)

Sub-section 3: The Laborers & The Foundations (Identity & Wages)

The construction of the Capitol serves as an accurate yet cruel mirror to American society and its economy in the first decades of its founding.

  • Identity & Quantity: Like the White House, building the original Capitol required between **400 to 600 laborers**. The primary and cheapest workforce consisted of **enslaved Black people** leased from Virginia plantation owners. They worked alongside free citizens (many of them European immigrants) who worked as expert builders, and Italian master carvers brought in to work the marble and give it classical grandeur. **The identity of the enslaved:** In the records, they were noted only by their first names under their owners. The most famous example is **Philip Reid**, a highly skilled enslaved Black man who was a key figure in casting and assembling the bronze panels of the "Statue of Freedom" that stands atop the dome.
  • Wages & Compensation: The disparity is highly evident in the federal Wage Rolls:
    • - **Master Artisans (Italians & British):** Earned massive salaries of **$1.50 to $2.50 per day**.
    • - **Free Laborers:** Earned about **50 to 75 cents per day**.
    • - **Enslaved Black People:** The US government paid a monthly rate of about **$5** – however, the entire sum was paid to the **lessor** (the plantation owner). The enslaved person who toiled in quarrying and logging received nothing for their labor.

Historical Irony: The "Statue of Freedom" placed atop the temple of democracy was assembled in 1863 by slaves while the Civil War raged in the background (fought over their right to freedom). Furthermore, when President Jefferson ordered the purchase of the land for the building, it was bought from a plantation owner and businessman who had named his plot "Rome", a historical curiosity that made Capitol Hill the modern Roman successor.

5. Destruction by Fire & Cultural Impact

The Great Fire of 1814: On August 24, 1814, British forces captured Washington D.C. After eating a victory meal at the White House, they marched to the Capitol building. They used the books of the "Library of Congress" (then located in the North Wing) as kindling and set the entire building ablaze. The stone walls survived thanks to a sudden torrential rainstorm that stopped the fire. Rebuilding took many years and led to its redesign.

Cultural Impact & "The Temple of Law": The Capitol represents the core of US political power (and influences the rest of the democratic world).

  • Presidential Inaugurations: Since the era of President Jackson, every American president takes the oath of office on the steps of the Capitol's West or East Front, facing hundreds of thousands of citizens gathered on The National Mall, as a symbol of the peaceful transfer of power.
  • Focal Point of Protests (January 6): On January 6, 2021, the building experienced its greatest internal threat when a mob of outgoing President Donald Trump's supporters stormed the building and breached its halls to disrupt the certification of the electoral vote. The traumatic event demonstrated, on one hand, the fragility of democracy and the building's vulnerability, and on the other, the resilience of the institutions as Congress returned to the chamber that same night to complete the certification process.

A view of the Capitol's facade and its massive dome. This building has served as the home of the House of Representatives and the Senate since 1800. Its design was heavily influenced by classical Greek and Roman architecture.

A Monument of Cast Iron (1861)

The new dome, designed by Thomas U. Walter, is built on a framework of nearly 9,000,000 pounds of iron. Despite the outbreak of the Civil War, President Lincoln ordered its construction to continue as a symbol that "the Union goes on."

Fire in the Capital (War of 1812)

On August 24, 1814, after their victory at the Battle of Bladensburg, the British marched into Washington D.C. and set the Capitol ablaze, using the books of Congress as kindling. Only a sudden torrential downpour prevented total collapse.
