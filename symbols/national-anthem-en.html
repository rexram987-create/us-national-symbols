The Star-Spangled Banner

Comprehensive Historical Research: The National Anthem

1. Historical Identity: Creation and Adoption

  • Date the Poem was Written: September 14, 1814.
  • Date Adopted as Official Anthem: March 3, 1931 (Signed by President Herbert Hoover).
  • Circumstances of its Writing (1814): During the War of 1812 (the second and final conflict between the US and Britain), the British invaded Washington D.C., setting fire to the White House and the Capitol. From there, they proceeded to the strategic port city of Baltimore. A young American lawyer, Francis Scott Key, boarded a British warship to negotiate the release of a captured American physician (Dr. William Beanes). The British agreed to release him but refused to let them leave, as they were privy to the British attack plan.
    For 25 consecutive hours, on September 13 and 14, 1814, Key watched from the British ship a brutal and relentless bombardment of Fort McHenry, which defended Baltimore. As dawn broke, through the gunpowder smoke, Key expected to see the British flag of surrender. Instead, he saw that the massive "garrison flag" (sewn by Mary Pickersgill) was still waving proudly. In a storm of emotion, he pulled an envelope from his pocket and wrote the poem "Defence of Fort M'Henry" on its back.
  • Circumstances of its Selection as National Anthem (1931): Amazingly, for over 150 years, the United States did not have an official anthem. Songs like "Hail, Columbia" and "My Country, 'Tis of Thee" served as unofficial anthems. Toward the end of the 19th century, the US Army and Navy began playing "The Star-Spangled Banner" at official ceremonies, and President Woodrow Wilson endorsed this practice in 1916.
    But the real catalyst for legislation was a public campaign: in 1929, the famous column "Ripley's Believe It or Not!" published a cartoon stating: "Believe It or Not, I can't sing the National Anthem because my country has none!" The publication caused a public uproar. Patriotic organizations collected over 5 million signatures demanding an anthem. Congress yielded to the pressure, and in 1931, the law was passed.

2. Etymological Research: The Creator and the Creation

  • The Star-Spangled Banner:
    • Star: Old English steorra.
    • Spangled: From Old English spang (a sparkling ornament). Adjective meaning: "decorated with glittering spots".
    • Banner: Late Latin bandum (a battle standard / symbolic piece of cloth).
  • Francis Scott Key:
    • Francis: Latin Franciscus. Meaning: a person belonging to the Frankish tribe, later synonymous with "free man" (Frank).
    • Scott: Anglo-Saxon Scotti. Originally a nickname for an Irish Celtic tribe that invaded Scotland. Meaning: Gaelic speaker or wanderer.
    • Key: Old English Cæg (key). As a surname, it was often given to the keeper of the keys of a town or important castle (a trusted official).

3. Francis Scott Key - The Man, Childhood, and Complex Ideology

  • Lifespan: August 1, 1779 – January 11, 1843.
  • Childhood and Education: Born at the family plantation "Terra Rubra" in Maryland to a wealthy and respected family. He received extensive classical education, attending St. John's College in Annapolis. He subsequently read law and became an attorney in Washington D.C.
  • Ideology and Contradictions (The Paradox of Liberty): Key embodies the American paradox of his time. On the one hand, he wrote the greatest hymn of praise to American freedom ("the land of the free"). On the other hand, he was a wealthy man who owned Black slaves.
    His ideology was complex and contradictory: as a deeply religious man (Episcopalian), he morally opposed the slave trade and often represented Black slaves in court for free to help them win their freedom. However, he opposed the total abolition of slavery (Abolitionism). He was a founding member of the "American Colonization Society" (ACS) – an ideological organization working to send freed slaves and free African Americans back to Africa (to a colony that later became the state of Liberia), believing that different races could not live together in equality in America.

The Original Designer of the Flag: Francis Hopkinson

It is important to note the connection between the anthem and the creator of the symbol it praises. The Star-Spangled Banner upon which the poem was written is based on Hopkinson's original design.

  • Lifespan: September 21, 1737 – May 9, 1791.
  • Childhood and Education (The Polymath): Francis was born in 1737 in Philadelphia to an influential family. His father, Thomas Hopkinson, was a prominent politician and partner of Benjamin Franklin. He was the first student and graduate of the College of Philadelphia (now UPenn). He acquired broad legal, musical, and artistic education, and is considered the first American composer to write secular music.
  • Ideology and the Satirical Aspect: Hopkinson believed culture and symbols were the foundation of national identity. He was a brilliant satirist during the Revolution, using his pen to mock the British Crown. His most famous satire, "A Pretty Story" (1774), allegorized the Britain-colonies relationship as a broken family. His satire was a powerful political mobilization tool aiding the push for independence.

4. The Melody (The Historical Irony)

Ironically, the famous American anthem symbolizing the victory over the British is sung to a British tune. The melody was written in 1773 by British composer John Stafford Smith, originally serving as a club song ("To Anacreon in Heaven") for a society of gentlemen music (and wine) lovers in London. The tune was very popular in America, and Key fitted the lyrics of his poem exactly to this rhythm.

5. The Full Anthem Lyrics and Historical Censorship

Why is only the first stanza sung?

When Francis Scott Key stood on the deck in 1814, he never intended to write a national anthem. He wrote a narrative poem designed to chronicle the entire 25 hours of battle. Therefore, the poem required four stanzas to complete the story arc (from anxiety at night, through discovery in the morning, fury at the enemy, to faith in God).

However, when the poem officially became the anthem in 1931, reality dictated two major constraints causing the "omission" of the last three stanzas in public use:

  • The Practical Constraint (Time): Singing all four stanzas at the anthem's solemn tempo takes about 5 minutes. This is too long for public ceremonies and sporting events.
  • The Diplomatic and Moral Constraint (The Third Stanza): The third stanza became a significant problem. It deeply insulted the British (who had become US allies). Furthermore, the line disparaging "hireling and slave" became jarring and racist in the post-Civil War era after the abolition of slavery.

Consequently, official protocol (to this day) dictates that public singing of the anthem includes only the first stanza (Stanza 1).

Here is the full, original text, including the stanzas hidden from public performance:

Stanza 1 (The Familiar and Sung Stanza): O say can you see, by the dawn's early light,
What so proudly we hail'd at the twilight's last gleaming,
Whose broad stripes and bright stars through the perilous fight
O'er the ramparts we watch'd were so gallantly streaming?
And the rocket's red glare, the bombs bursting in air,
Gave proof through the night that our flag was still there,
O say does that star-spangled banner yet wave
O'er the land of the free and the home of the brave?
Stanza 2 (The Answer and Discovery): On the shore dimly seen through the mists of the deep
Where the foe's haughty host in dread silence reposes,
What is that which the breeze, o'er the towering steep,
As it fitfully blows, half conceals, half discloses?
Now it catches the gleam of the morning's first beam,
In full glory reflected now shines in the stream,
'Tis the star-spangled banner - O long may it wave
O'er the land of the free and the home of the brave!
Stanza 3 (The Fury and the Controversial Stanza): And where is that band who so vauntingly swore,
That the havoc of war and the battle's confusion
A home and a Country should leave us no more?
Their blood has wash'd out their foul footstep's pollution.
No refuge could save the hireling and slave
From the terror of flight or the gloom of the grave,
And the star-spangled banner in triumph doth wave
O'er the land of the free and the home of the brave.
Stanza 4 (The Stanza of Victory and Faith): O thus be it ever when freemen shall stand
Between their lov'd home and the war's desolation!
Blest with vict'ry and peace may the heav'n rescued land
Praise the power that hath made and preserv'd us a nation!
Then conquer we must, when our cause it is just,
And this be our motto - "In God is our trust,"
And the star-spangled banner in triumph shall wave
O'er the land of the free and the home of the brave.
