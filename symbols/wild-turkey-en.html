The Wild Turkey

The original American bird: from the pioneers' feast, to Benjamin Franklin's myth, to the greatest linguistic confusion in history.

Zoological Identification

  • Scientific Name: Meleagris gallopavo
  • Family: Phasianidae (Pheasants)
  • Native Habitat: North America (from the US to Northern Mexico). The turkey is an original, endemic North American bird, unlike regular chickens whose origins trace back to Asia.
  • Dimensions & Weight: There is pronounced sexual dimorphism (a significant difference between males and females). Adult males (Toms or Gobblers) weigh between 11 to 24 lbs (5-11 kg), are 39-49 inches (100-125 cm) long, and boast an impressive wingspan of 4.1 to 4.7 feet (1.25-1.44 m). Females (Hens) are significantly smaller and lighter, weighing 5.5 to 12 lbs (2.5-5.4 kg) and measuring 30-37 inches (76-95 cm) in length.
  • Diet: The wild turkey is an opportunistic omnivore that primarily forages on the forest floor. Its diet changes with the seasons and includes acorns and nuts (a crucial source of fat for the winter), seeds, berries, roots, as well as insects, spiders, and even small reptiles and amphibians like lizards and frogs.
  • Conservation Status: Currently listed as "Least Concern" (LC) on the IUCN Red List. This is one of the greatest ecological success stories in North America. By the early 20th century, due to unchecked commercial hunting and habitat loss, the population plummeted to merely ~30,000 birds, teetering on the brink of extinction. Thanks to conservation laws, the establishment of wildlife refuges, and organizations like the National Wild Turkey Federation (NWTF), the population recovered dramatically and today numbers over 6 million in the wild.

The Great Etymological Confusion

The name of this bird is the result of one of the funniest geographical mistakes in the history of languages:

Why is it called "Turkey" in English?

In the 16th century, Turkish merchants (from the Ottoman Empire) imported African birds called "Guinea fowl" into England. The English called these birds "Turkey coqs" (Turkish birds). Later, when English settlers arrived in America and saw the American wild turkey, they thought it looked similar to the "Turkish" bird they knew back home, and mistakenly called it "Turkey" as well. Thus, an entirely American bird received the name of a Middle Eastern country.

Why is it called "Hodu" (India) in Hebrew?

In Israel (and in other languages like French: Dinde, and Russian: Hindi), the mistake stems from repeating Christopher Columbus's famous error. Because the bird came from the American continent, which was mistakenly thought to be "India" (the West Indies), it was dubbed the "bird from India" – or in Hebrew, Tarnegol Hodu (India Rooster). Thus, the exact same bird received the name of an Asian country in other languages.

Folklore: The Symbol of Thanksgiving

  • The Pilgrims' Feast (1621): A famous myth claims that turkey was the main dish at the first Thanksgiving feast shared by the Pilgrims and the Native Americans (the Wampanoag) in Plymouth. Historically, there is no conclusive proof of this; contemporary records merely mention "wild fowl" and venison (deer meat).
  • Shaping the Holiday Symbol (19th Century): The turkey only became the official centerpiece of the holiday in the 19th century. The reasons were highly practical: it was a native American bird, massive in size (enough to feed an entire extended family), and it had no other commercial utility since it doesn't lay eggs reliably on a commercial scale and doesn't produce milk (unlike cows and regular chickens).
  • The Presidential Pardon: Since the mid-20th century, an amusing cultural tradition has developed wherein the President of the United States grants a "Presidential Pardon" to one or two turkeys at the White House the day before Thanksgiving, saving them from the national dinner plate. George H.W. Bush was the first to make it a formalized, permanent tradition.

The Benjamin Franklin Myth

One of the most famous historical myths is that Benjamin Franklin fought for the turkey to be the national bird of America instead of the Bald Eagle. The truth is slightly different, but no less fascinating:

Franklin never actually proposed the turkey as a national symbol before Congress. However, in a private letter written to his daughter (Sarah Bache) in 1784, he harshly criticized the initial design of the Great Seal (in which the bald eagle supposedly looked a bit like a turkey). In his letter, he argued that the Bald Eagle is a "bird of bad moral character" because it steals food from other birds (the Osprey) and is a "rank coward."

In contrast, Franklin humorously wrote, the turkey is "a much more respectable Bird, and withal a true original Native of America... He is besides, though a little vain & silly, a Bird of Courage, and would not hesitate to attack a Grenadier of the British Guards who should presume to invade his Farm Yard with a red Coat on." Although it was just a joke in a personal letter, the idea became entrenched in American folklore as Franklin's "lost proposal."

The Wild Turkey

A wild male (Tom) in all its glory. Unlike domestic, clumsy turkeys, the wild breed is athletic, boasts brilliant copper-colored iridescent feathers, and possesses impressive short-distance flight capabilities.

The Thanksgiving Feast

A classic illustration of a Thanksgiving dinner. Although it is doubtful it was eaten at the first Pilgrim feast, the turkey became the central and iconic dish of American folklore in the 19th century.

Benjamin Franklin and the "Courageous" Bird

In a letter to his daughter, Franklin humorously argued that the turkey would have been a better choice than the bald eagle, as it is a "true original Native of America" that would not hesitate to attack a British soldier.
