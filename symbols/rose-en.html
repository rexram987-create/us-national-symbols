Comprehensive Historical Research: The Rose – The National Floral Emblem

A symbol of life, beauty, and devotion: from George Washington's breeding to the historic presidential proclamation.

1. Historical Identity: Year of Adoption and the Reason for Selection

  • Year of Official Declaration and Adoption: November 20, 1986. President Ronald Reagan signed Proclamation 5574, establishing the rose as the national floral emblem of the United States. Symbolically, the signing ceremony was held in the famous White House Rose Garden.
  • Reason for Adoption: In his proclamation, Reagan noted: "More often than any other flower, we hold the rose dear as the symbol of life and love and devotion, of beauty and eternity... We see proofs of this everywhere. The study of fossils reveals that the rose has existed in America for age upon age. We have always cultivated roses in our gardens." In addition to the emotional reason, the rose has a physical presence in all 50 US states, growing both in cultivated gardens and in the American wild.

2. Etymological Research: Rose

  • Rose:
    • The English word came directly from the Classical Latin Rosa. The origin of the Latin name is likely a borrowing from Ancient Greek: Rhódon (ῥόδον).
  • The Ancient Persian Origin:
    • Many linguists believe the Greeks borrowed the word from Old Persian, where the word Wurdi (or Varda) simply meant "flower." This is also the source of the Aramaic-Persian name that evolved into the modern Hebrew word for rose – Vered.
  • The Historical Confusion with "Lily" (Shoshana):
    • In Jewish and Israeli culture, there is a common confusion between the rose and the lily. The biblical "Shoshana" ("like a lily among thorns") likely refers to a flower from the Liliaceae family or a tulip. However, during the Middle Ages in Europe, Jewish poets began translating the word "Rose" to the Hebrew word "Shoshana," and thus the confusion became rooted in the vernacular to this day.

3. Historical Context: The Founding Fathers and the Presidential Rose Garden

Beyond being an ornamental flower, the rose is deeply intertwined with American history, from the nation's founders to the White House:

  • George Washington (The First Rose Breeder): The first President of the United States was not only a military commander but also an avid botanist. At his estate, Mount Vernon, he engaged in crossbreeding and cultivating roses, even developing and naming a special rose variety after his mother: the "Mary Washington Rose," a variety that continues to bloom today.
  • The White House Rose Garden: Although previous presidents had planted roses around the White House, the official Rose Garden was first established in 1913 by First Lady Ellen Wilson. However, the iconic design we know today—which serves as the most prestigious stage for presidential speeches and press conferences—was planned in 1961 during the tenure of President John F. Kennedy.
The Designer: Rachel "Bunny" Mellon

Kennedy enlisted top horticulturist Rachel Lambert "Bunny" Mellon to redesign the garden so it would serve as an open, spectacular space for diplomatic and national hosting. Her design combined wide lawns and precise plantings of roses, creating the classic look associated with the presidency to this day.

4. Botanical and Physical Characteristics

  • Geological History: Fossils found in North America, primarily in Colorado and Oregon, prove that wild roses grew on the American continent over 35 million years ago.
  • Species and Varieties: There are over 150 different biological species of roses in the world, and among them, dozens are native to North America.
  • "No Rose Without Thorns" – A Scientific Error: Botanically speaking, what we call "thorns" on a rose stem are actually prickles. Unlike true thorns, which are modified stems (like on a lemon tree), the prickles of a rose are outgrowths of the epidermis (the plant's skin layer), and their function is protection against herbivores and assisting in climbing and clinging.
