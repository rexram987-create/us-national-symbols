The Declaration of Independence

The document that founded the nation, created the American Creed, and changed the course of world history.

Historical Identity: Origin & Evolution

  • Adoption and Recognition: The Declaration was officially adopted by the Second Continental Congress on July 4, 1776. Contrary to popular myth, most delegates did not sign it on July 4th, but rather on August 2, 1776.
  • Reason for Adoption: The Revolutionary War had begun a year prior. The Declaration served three primary purposes: to explain to the world (especially France) why the rebellion was legally and morally justified, to unite the 13 colonies under a shared cause, and to sever political ties with Great Britain permanently.

Deconstructed Etymological Research

Declaration

Originates from Latin: Declarare (to make clear, to reveal). It is composed of De- (completely) and Clarare (to clarify/illuminate). The profound literal meaning is exposing the political truth to the light of day.

Independence

Composed of the Latin prefix In- (not) and the verb Dependere (to hang from, or rely upon). The literal meaning is "not to rely on another"—to stand on one's own two feet.

Thomas Jefferson

  • Thomas: An ancient Aramaic name, "Ta'oma", which translates literally to "Twin".
  • Jefferson: An English patronymic surname meaning "Son of Jeffrey". Jeffrey derives from Old Germanic roots (Gaufrid), meaning "God's peace".

The People Behind the Symbol

Thomas Jefferson (1743 – 1826) - Principal Author

Early Life & Education: Born in Virginia to a wealthy planter family. Jefferson was a true polymath: an architect, philosopher, scientist, and linguist. He avidly read the European Enlightenment thinkers, particularly John Locke, from whom he borrowed the concept of "natural rights."

Ideology & The American Paradox: Jefferson was only 33 when he drafted the document. He penned the most revolutionary and moral words in human history ("all men are created equal"). Yet herein lies his greatest paradox: Jefferson owned hundreds of slaves at his Monticello plantation and, with few exceptions, never freed them.

John Hancock

President of the Continental Congress. He was the first to sign the Declaration, doing so in huge, flamboyant letters. Legend says he signed it so large "so King George could read it without his spectacles." To this day, the term "John Hancock" is American slang for a signature.

Anatomy of the Document

The Declaration is not a legal blueprint like the Constitution, but rather a moral philosophy and an indictment. It is divided into several parts:

  • The Preamble: Here, Jefferson formulates the moral justification for rebellion. It contains the most famous sentence ever written in the English language regarding human rights.
  • Grievances: This makes up the bulk of the document, listing 27 specific abuses committed by King George III (taxation without consent, quartering troops, abolishing laws).
  • Final Declaration: The conclusion where the colonies officially declare themselves "Free and Independent States."

Cultural Impact and Meaning

If the Constitution is the "law", the Declaration is the "American Creed". It was initially written merely to justify a separation from Britain, but the promise that "all men are created equal" took on a life of its own:

  • Abraham Lincoln invoked it in his Gettysburg Address to prove the nation was born in 1776 around the concept of equality.
  • Martin Luther King Jr. quoted it in his "I Have a Dream" speech (1963), calling it a "promissory note" that America needed to cash for its Black citizens.
