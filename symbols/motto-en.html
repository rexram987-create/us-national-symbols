In God We Trust

The historical roots of the national motto, its theological significance, and the religious battles that shaped American currency.

Historical Identity: From Two Mottos to One

  • The First Unofficial Motto (1782): With the establishment of the Republic, the Founding Fathers chose the Latin phrase "E Pluribus Unum" (Out of many, one) as a de facto motto. It reflected their political vision of uniting 13 separate colonies into one strong nation, without religious reference.
  • Year of First Appearance (1864): The phrase "In God We Trust" first appeared on American currency (a two-cent coin) during the bloody Civil War, following public demand to place God's name on the nation's coins during times of crisis.
  • Reason for Official Adoption (1956): On July 30, 1956, during the Cold War, President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed a law making "In God We Trust" the sole official national motto of the US. The main reason was ideological: a strong desire to distinguish American democracy (based on freedom of religion and belief) from Soviet communism, which promoted state atheism.

Deconstructed Etymological Research

E Pluribus Unum

A Latin phrase. Composed of the word Ex/E (Out of), the word Pluribus (Many - plural form), and the word Unum (One). The origin of the phrase is likely a poem by the Roman poet Virgil about making a salad (Moretum), where many ingredients merge into one color and flavor.

In God We Trust

An English phrase. The word God derives from Proto-Germanic (Gudan), meaning "the entity invoked". The word Trust originates from Old Norse (Traust), meaning confidence, comfort, or strong protection. Interestingly, the phrase is directly influenced by the fourth stanza of the National Anthem ("And this be our motto: 'In God is our trust'").

The People Behind the Symbol

Salmon P. Chase - Father of the Currency

Etymology:
  • Salmon: Often associated with the fish (from Latin Salire, to leap), but as a first name, it is usually an English version of the biblical Hebrew name "Shalmon" or "Solomon" (Peaceable).
  • Chase: An Old French surname (Chacier) meaning "to hunt" or "the hunter".

Childhood & Background: Chase was born in 1808 in New Hampshire. After his father's death, he was raised by his uncle, who was a strict Episcopalian bishop. This deep religious upbringing shaped his moral and political worldview.

Ideology and Role: Chase served as Secretary of the Treasury in Abraham Lincoln's cabinet during the Civil War. He was a staunch abolitionist and a very religious man. In 1861, following appeals from clergymen arguing that the nation needed divine help in the bloody war, Chase instructed the Mint Director to add God's name to the nation's coins, stating: "No nation can be strong except in the strength of God, or safe except in His defense."

Dwight D. Eisenhower - The Legislator

Etymology:
  • Dwight: Derived from an old Germanic/Dutch name meaning "blond/white", or from the name Diot meaning "people".
  • Eisenhower: A name of German origin (Eisenhauer). Composed of Eisen (iron) and hauer (hewer/striker). Meaning: "Iron hewer" or "iron miner".

Childhood & Background: Born in Texas but raised in Abilene, Kansas (1890). He grew up in a very religious, pacifist family (his parents were members of a sect that later evolved into Jehovah's Witnesses). Despite his family's pacifist background, he chose a military career and became the general who led the Allied invasion of Europe in WWII (D-Day).

Ideology and Role: As the 34th President of the US, Eisenhower believed the war against the Soviet Union was not merely military or economic, but a moral and spiritual war. He led a public religious awakening in America. Under his leadership in 1954, it was decided to add the words "Under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance, and in 1956 he signed the historic law making "In God We Trust" the official national motto, directing it to be printed on all paper currency.

Anatomy of the Symbol

Since the motto is not a physical object but a statement, its anatomy is expressed in the places where it is applied and displayed to the general public:

  • On Coins: Since 1864, the motto gradually began to appear on various coins (cent, nickel, dollar). Today it is mandatory on every American coin produced by the Mint.
  • On Paper Currency: The motto did not appear on paper currency until 1957. The first bill to bear the motto on its back was a one-dollar silver certificate. By 1966, the motto was added to all denominations of American currency.
  • In Public Buildings: The motto is engraved in large gold letters on the wall of the House of Representatives chamber, directly above the Speaker's rostrum. It also appears in many courthouses, including the Supreme Court, as a symbol of divine providence over the law.

Cultural Impact and Meaning

The motto is considered one of the most controversial symbols in American history, as it sits right on the seam line of the "First Amendment" (freedom of religion and the separation of church and state):

  • Lawsuits: Over the decades, dozens of lawsuits have been filed by secular and atheist organizations demanding the removal of the motto from American currency, arguing that it violates the separation of church and state and discriminates against citizens who do not believe in God.
  • Court Rulings: Federal courts in the US, as well as the Supreme Court (indirectly), have consistently rejected all lawsuits. The accepted legal justification is that the motto has lost its pure theological meaning and has become a concept of "Ceremonial Deism" - a secular and traditional term meant to evoke historical patriotism rather than promote a specific religion.
  • Heritage vs. Modernity: Today, many US states (especially in the South and conservative ones) are passing laws requiring the motto "In God We Trust" to be prominently displayed in every public school in the state, which continues to ignite fierce cultural debates between conservatives who see it as foundational heritage, and liberals who view "E Pluribus Unum" as the authentic, unifying motto of the Founders' vision.

E Pluribus Unum

The original unofficial motto, "Out of many, one," as it appears on the Great Seal of the United States, symbolizing the union of the 13 colonies.

In God We Trust

The motto as it appears on American coinage. The original addition was intended to emphasize that the nation relied on a higher power during the crises of the Civil War.

President Dwight D. Eisenhower

Eisenhower, the 34th President, made "In God We Trust" the official motto of the United States in 1956, as a distinct ideological move against Soviet Communism.
