Comprehensive Historical Research: The Bald Eagle – The Symbol of American Power

Click on the image to enlarge

1. Historical Identity: Year of Adoption and the Reason for Selection

  • Year of Official Declaration and Adoption: The Bald Eagle was officially adopted as the national emblem of the United States on June 20, 1782. This decision coincided with the approval of the final design for the Great Seal by the Continental Congress, where the eagle occupies the central position.
  • Reason for Invention and Adoption: The young nation, newly liberated from the British Empire, desperately sought a visual symbol that would project power, sovereignty, and uncompromising liberty. Historically, eagles have served as symbols of power throughout almost all of Western history, from the Roman Republic (the "Aquila") through the Holy Roman Empire, and to Tsarist Russia.
    However, the American Founding Fathers wanted to disconnect from the old European symbolism, which was often associated with monarchies and oppression. The choice of the Bald Eagle was a distinct strategic and tactical decision: unlike European eagles, the Bald Eagle is a species endemic (unique) only to the North American continent. This choice was intended to declare to the world that the United States was not merely a "copy" of a European country, but an independent, wild, free entity with a unique identity of its own.

2. Etymological Research: Bald Eagle

The name "Bald Eagle" is often a source of confusion, since the eagle's head, once it reaches maturity (around 4-5 years of age), is crowned with bright white feathers and is not bald at all.

  • Bald: In its historical origin, the word "Bald" does not refer to a lack of hair or feathers. It derives from the Old English word Balde (or Balled), whose original and exact meaning was "white" or "having a bright white patch". This usage is preserved in old English terms like "Piebald", which describes an animal (like a horse) with prominent black and white patches. The true historical meaning of the name is, therefore, "The eagle with the bright white head".
  • Eagle: Derived from the Old French word Aigle, which itself evolved from the Latin word Aquila (eagle). Some scholars believe that in ancient Latin, the root meaning of the word relates to a dark color, black or brown-black, referring to the dark body color of these birds of prey.
  • The Scientific Latin Name (Haliaeetus leucocephalus):
    • The first part, Haliaeetus, comes from Ancient Greek: Halos means sea or salt, and Aetos means eagle. The combination creates "sea eagle".
    • The second part, leucocephalus, is also from Ancient Greek: Leukos means white, and Kephale means head.
    • Etymological Synthesis of the Scientific Name: "The white-headed sea eagle".

3. The Designers and Thinkers: Charles Thomson and William Barton

Positioning the Bald Eagle as the central symbol of the nation was not the brilliant stroke of a single individual, but an ideological combination of two key figures who played active roles in the third and final committee for designing the Great Seal:

  • Charles Thomson:
    • Lifespan: November 29, 1729 – August 16, 1824.
    • Role and Influence: Thomson served as the Secretary of the Continental Congress and was considered a polymath. He was the man who took the scattered and unformulated ideas of the two previous committees and organized them into one coherent design. His critical contribution was the decision to place the American eagle at center stage, proudly and sovereignly spreading its wings, holding an olive branch (symbolizing peace) in one talon, and a bundle of arrows (symbolizing war) in the other.
  • William Barton:
    • Lifespan: April 11, 1754 – October 21, 1817.
    • Role and Influence: A lawyer and an expert in heraldry (the study of coats of arms and aristocratic symbols). Barton was actually the first to officially propose incorporating an eagle into the national emblem. However, in his original proposal, he referred to a small European-style crested eagle or a traditional heraldic eagle. Thomson took Barton's idea and refined it, taking care to specifically change the bird to the "American Bald Eagle" – to ensure unique geographic representation and total detachment from Europe.

4. The Ideological Polemic: Benjamin Franklin and the Turkey

One of the most fascinating and well-known stories (though sometimes slightly distorted) surrounding the adoption of the symbol is the fierce ideological opposition of Benjamin Franklin.

  • Lifespan: January 17, 1706 – April 17, 1790.
  • The Moral Critique of the Eagle: In a famous letter written to his daughter Sarah (Sarah Bache) in 1784 – two full years after Congress had officially adopted the symbol – Franklin expressed open dissatisfaction with the choice. He argued that the Bald Eagle is "a bird of bad moral character". Franklin justified this by stating that the Bald Eagle does not always bother to hunt its own food; instead, it often perches on dead trees by the river and steals fish from smaller Osprey birds that have worked hard to fish. Franklin disdainfully called the eagle "a rank coward".
  • The Ideological Alternative - The Wild Turkey: In the same letter, Franklin suggested that the wild turkey is a much more respectable and true American symbol. Franklin admitted that the turkey might look a little clumsy or "vain," but he argued that it is "a bird of courage," a native bird that would not hesitate to attack British soldiers (Grenadiers) in red coats who dared to invade its territory or farm.
  • Important Research Note to Prevent Myths: It is important to emphasize that Franklin never proposed the turkey as an official symbol during the Congressional debates. He shared his satirical, stinging, and ideological opinion solely within a private correspondence. Nonetheless, the letter sheds fascinating light on how the Founding Fathers scrutinized every symbol through the lens of morality and politics.

5. Symbolism and National Ideology Behind the Eagle

Ultimately, the choice of the Bald Eagle proved highly successful and provided the United States with exactly the visual identity required at the time:

  1. Limitless Freedom: The Bald Eagle flies at immense heights and is considered to have virtually no natural predators in the sky. This trait symbolizes a completely independent, free nation subject to no one.
  2. Longevity and Endurance: Eagles are known to be long-lived animals (capable of living decades in the wild). This fact symbolizes the Founders' hope and belief that the new Republic would survive and prosper forever, unlike the empires of the past that collapsed.
  3. Separation of Powers and Deterrence: The very fact that in the national seal, the eagle holds the symbols of war (arrows) and peace (olive branch) in its two talons, symbolizes a delicate balance of power. It is crucial to note that the eagle's head is turned toward the olive branch – a clear hint that the United States prefers a sincere desire for peace, but is prepared and ready to defend itself with lethal military force (the arrows) if necessary.
