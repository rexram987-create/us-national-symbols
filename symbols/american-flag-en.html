Comprehensive Historical Research: The United States Flag, Francis Hopkinson, and the Story of Betsy Ross

Click on images to enlarge

The American flag, recognized today as one of the most prominent and influential visual symbols in the world, is the product of continuous historical evolution rather than a single moment of magic.

The United States Flag (The American Flag)

  • Year of Design and Official Adoption: The first official flag (known as the "13-Star Flag") was approved by the Second Continental Congress on June 14, 1777 (a date celebrated today as "Flag Day").
  • Reason for Invention and Adoption: With the outbreak of the American Revolutionary War (1775), the Continental Army under the command of George Washington fought under a variety of local flags representing different colonies and units. This situation created dangerous confusion on the battlefield. The Congress realized the urgent tactical and moral need for a uniform national flag that would unite the 13 colonies, provide clear identification at sea (for the young Navy) and on land, and serve as a sovereign symbol declaring total separation from the British Crown (since the previous "Grand Union" flag still included the British flag in its canton).
  • The Evolution of the Design: The Flag Act of 1777 merely stipulated that there should be 13 alternating red and white stripes, and 13 white stars on a blue field, representing a "new constellation". The act did not specify how the stars should be arranged, resulting in various versions (in a circle, in rows, in an arch). Today, the flag features 50 stars (the 50th state, Hawaii, was added in 1959) and 13 stripes, which are preserved in memory of the original colonies.

The Original Designer: Francis Hopkinson

Although popular folklore attributes the design of the flag to Betsy Ross, historical documents in the National Archives indicate that the person who actually designed and planned the flag was Francis Hopkinson.

  • Lifespan: September 21, 1737 – May 9, 1791.
  • Name Etymology:
    • Francis: Derived from the Latin word Franciscus, literally meaning "Frenchman" or "a person belonging to the Frankish tribe". In English, the name became synonymous with the concept of being Frank - meaning a free, honest, and forthright person.
    • Hopkinson: An English patronymic surname (named after the father). Hopkin is a diminutive of Hob (which itself is a shortcut for the name Robert - of Germanic origin meaning "bright fame"), combined with the suffix son. The literal meaning: "Son of the glorious little Robert".
  • Childhood and Education (The Polymath): Francis was born in 1737 in Philadelphia to a family of status and influence. His father, Thomas Hopkinson, was a prominent politician and an associate of Benjamin Franklin. He was the first student and first graduate of the College of Philadelphia (now the University of Pennsylvania - UPenn). He acquired a broad legal, musical, and artistic education, and is even considered the first American composer to write secular music.
  • Ideology and the Satirical Aspect: Hopkinson believed that culture and symbols were the foundation of national identity. He was one of the most brilliant satirists of the Revolutionary era, using his pen as a weapon to mock the British Crown. His most famous satirical work, "A Pretty Story" (1774), presented the relationship between Britain and the colonies as an allegory of a broken family where the father (King George III) abuses his children. His satire was a powerful political mobilization tool that helped shape public opinion in favor of independence. His flag design was historically confirmed via a letter demanding payment ("a quarter cask of the public wine") sent to Congress.

The Name: The Star-Spangled Banner (History and Etymology)

The familiar name of the flag was not born in 1777, but 37 years later, under dramatic circumstances. On the morning of September 14, 1814, during the War of 1812, a lawyer and poet named Francis Scott Key witnessed a brutal British naval bombardment of Fort McHenry in Baltimore. As dawn broke, through the gunpowder smoke, he saw that the American flag (then in a 15-star and 15-stripe version) was still proudly waving. This sight inspired him to write a poem, which became a hit and eventually (in 1931) the official National Anthem. Since then, the public has referred to the flag by the poem's name.

  • The: The definite article, singularizing the object.
  • Star: From the Old English steorra and Proto-Germanic sternon.
  • Spangled: The root Spangle (Old English spang) refers to a sparkling metallic ornament. Spangled is the adjective form meaning: "decorated or adorned with glittering spots."
  • Banner: Originating from Old French baniere, and Late Latin bandum (a piece of cloth, a standard raised in battle).
  • Synthesis: The literal and exact meaning is "The flag adorned with glittering star points."

Betsy Ross - The Woman and the Legend

  • Full Name: Elizabeth Griscom. Known as Betsy Ross after her first husband, John Ross.
  • Lifespan: January 1, 1752 – January 30, 1836.
  • Name Etymology:
    • Elizabeth: From Biblical Hebrew, "Elisheva". "Eli" (My God) and "Sheva" (Oath/Abundance). The meaning: "God is my oath". Betsy is a common diminutive for this name.
    • Griscom: A toponymic surname (named after a place) from England. Derived from the words Gris (gray in Norman French) and Combe (valley in Old English), meaning: "The Gray Valley".
    • Ross: Of Celtic origin, Ros, meaning a peninsula, a promontory, or a forest clearing.
  • Childhood and Ideology: Betsy was born in Philadelphia to a Quaker family. Raised with values of simplicity, modesty, and manual labor, she learned the trade of sewing and upholstery. Despite her strict upbringing, she displayed an ideology of freedom and independent thought: at age 21, she eloped to marry John Ross (who was Episcopalian), resulting in her permanent expulsion from the Quaker community and her family. This rebellious act aligned with the independent spirit of the Revolution.
  • Achievements and the Legend: After her husband was killed early in the Revolutionary War (1776), Betsy took over the business—an unusual achievement for a young woman at the time—and manufactured flags for the Navy and Army for about 50 years. The famous legend (propagated by her grandson in 1870) tells that in June 1776, George Washington arrived at her shop with a sketch of a flag featuring six-pointed stars. Betsy proved to him that a five-pointed star could be cut with a single quick snip of the scissors, and she was the one who sewed the first flag with the 13 stars in a circle (so no single colony would be prominent above the others). While parts of the legend are disputed by historians, her skill as a patriotic flag maker is a well-established fact, and she has become a powerful national symbol of the "Founding Mothers'" contributions to the establishment of the Republic.
