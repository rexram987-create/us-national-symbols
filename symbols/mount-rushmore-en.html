Mount Rushmore

1927–1941 | Shrine of Democracy

HE

Mount Rushmore

The granite-carved Shrine of Democracy: a megalomaniacal vision, an engineering marvel, and an open wound in the sacred land of Native Americans.

1. Historical Identity: From Tourist Trap to National Monument

  • Years of Construction: The project began on October 4, 1927, and concluded on October 31, 1941 (14 years of work).
  • The Creators: South Dakota historian Doane Robinson, who conceived the idea, and the American sculptor Gutzon Borglum, who designed and directed the carving.
  • Original Reason for Construction: Robinson's original idea in 1923 was purely economic and provincial: he sought a way to attract tourists to the isolated state of South Dakota. Robinson proposed carving Wild West heroes (like Lewis and Clark, Buffalo Bill Cody, and the Native Chief Red Cloud) into the granite rocks.
  • Borglum's Vision: When Robinson hired sculptor Gutzon Borglum, Borglum refused to sculpt regional figures. He argued that a project of such magnitude (an entire mountain) must have immense national significance. He decided that Mount Rushmore would be dedicated as the "Shrine of Democracy," focusing on the four presidents who shaped the United States from its birth to its rise as a global power.

2. Deconstructed Etymological Research

Mount Rushmore

The mountain is not named after a president, a war hero, or a Native American deity, but after a corporate lawyer from New York. In 1885, attorney Charles E. Rushmore was sent to South Dakota to check mining claims. Upon noticing the impressive mountain, he asked his local guide for its name. The guide replied that it essentially had no name, jokingly adding: "We will name it Rushmore." The unofficial name stuck and was officially recognized by the US Board on Geographic Names in 1930.

Deconstructing the name Rushmore: From Old English roots. Rush (a marsh plant, Risc) and More (or Moor - an open, uncultivated tract of land). Meaning: "The open land of rushes/reeds."

3. The People Behind the Symbol (Biography, Ideology, and Achievements)

Doane Robinson - The Visionary ("Father of Mount Rushmore")

Etymology:
  • Doane: From Celtic origin (Dubhán), meaning "dark" or "black." Often used as a surname that became a given name.
  • Robinson: A patronymic name meaning "son of Robin." Robin is a diminutive of Robert, meaning "bright fame" in ancient Germanic.

Lifespan: 1856 – 1946.

Childhood & Background: Born in Wisconsin to a farming family. Despite losing his father and sister at a young age, he acquired a legal education, but his true passion was history. He was appointed the official state historian of South Dakota.

Ideology: Robinson believed in the power of local history to generate regional pride and economic prosperity. He sought creative ways to turn his isolated, poor state into a national attraction by glorifying figures of the "Wild West."

Achievements & Role: Robinson is the man who conceived the idea of carving giant statues into the granite mountains of the Black Hills in 1923, to draw tourists (and their cars) to the region. His original plan was to carve regional figures like Buffalo Bill in what was called "The Needles." He was the one who hired sculptor Gutzon Borglum and rallied federal political support that made the project a reality.

Gutzon Borglum - The Master Sculptor

Etymology:
  • Gutzon: A given name of Scandinavian origin, a variation of "Gudbrand" meaning "God's sword" or divine gift.
  • Borglum: A toponymic surname from Denmark, named after "Børglum Abbey" in northern Jutland, Denmark.

Childhood & Background: Born in 1867 in the Idaho Territory to a father who was a Danish immigrant and a polygamist Mormon (married to two sisters). Gutzon, who later distanced himself from religion, grew up in the Wild West and traveled to Paris to study sculpture and art (where he was deeply influenced by Auguste Rodin).

Ideology and Conflict: Borglum was a fierce American nationalist, a passionate believer in "Manifest Destiny," and convinced of America's need to create colossal art that would surpass the pyramids in Egypt. However, his character is deeply controversial: prior to Mount Rushmore, he worked on the carving project at Stone Mountain in Georgia, intended to commemorate Confederate heroes of the South. During this period, he became a friend and closely associated with the Ku Klux Klan (KKK), which funded part of the project. (He was later fired from Stone Mountain and moved on to Mount Rushmore).

Achievements: Borglum conceived the national vision for Mount Rushmore and the use of dynamite as the primary "sculpting tool." He died in March 1941, just months before the work was completed, and his son, Lincoln Borglum, took command and officially finished the project in October of that year.

Charles E. Rushmore - The Namesake

Etymology:
  • Charles: From the Old Germanic name Karl, meaning "free man" or "man."
  • Rushmore: An English topographic origin meaning "the wild land of rushes."

Lifespan: 1857 – 1931.

Childhood & Background: Born in New York to a well-off family and received an excellent education. He became a highly successful and wealthy corporate attorney in Manhattan, New York, representing large commercial enterprises, including mining companies.

Ideology: Unlike Robinson and Borglum, who were men of vision and national ideology, Rushmore was a pragmatic businessman who represented the economic interests of the mining industry expanding westward.

Achievements & Role: In 1885, Rushmore was sent to South Dakota to verify legal claims on tin mines. While touring the area, he asked his local guide the name of the impressive mountain before them. The guide jokingly replied that it had no name, and therefore "from now on we will call it Mount Rushmore." The unofficial name stuck with the local population and was officially recognized in 1930. Rushmore did not object to the honor and even donated $5,000 out of his own pocket (a massive sum in those days) toward the construction of the sculptures, becoming one of the project's largest and earliest private donors.

4. Anatomy & Engineering: Carving Stone with Dynamite

Technical Specs of the Mountain and Faces:

  • Face Dimensions: Each of the presidents' faces stands 60 feet (18 meters) tall—equivalent to a 6-story building. Their eyes are about 11 feet (3.3 meters) wide, and their noses are 20 feet (6 meters) long.
  • The Sculpting Tool - Dynamite: Unlike classical sculptors who used only hammers and chisels, Borglum developed an unprecedented method. About 90% of the rock removed from the mountain (approximately 450,000 tons of granite) was removed using highly precise dynamite explosions. The blasts came within inches of the final skin of the statue. Only the final finishing was done by workers using pneumatic hammers and a process called "honeycombing."
  • Life on the Mountain: The project employed nearly 400 workers, mostly local miners with no artistic experience. Despite the terrifyingly dangerous conditions (hanging from harnesses and steel cables hundreds of feet in the air with loose dynamite), not a single worker died during the 14 years of work on the project.

The Four Presidents and Symbolism

Borglum personally selected the four presidents to represent 150 years of American history (from birth to preservation):

  1. George Washington: Represents the birth of the United States as an independent republic.
  2. Thomas Jefferson: Represents expansion and growth (as the author of the Declaration of Independence and purchaser of the Louisiana Territory, which doubled the nation's size).
  3. Theodore Roosevelt: Represents economic development, the construction of the Panama Canal, and the nature conservation movement in the 20th century (chosen specifically because he was a personal friend of Borglum).
  4. Abraham Lincoln: Represents the preservation (survival) of the Union following the crisis of the Civil War and the moral abolition of slavery.

The Secret Room: Hall of Records

Borglum's original vision was that a tunnel would be carved behind the presidents' faces leading to a massive hall, a sort of "time capsule" or sacred vault, where the original copies of the U.S. Constitution and the Declaration of Independence would be preserved for generations thousands of years from now. Work on the tunnel (behind Lincoln's head) began in 1938, but Congress ordered Borglum to stop immediately and channel all funding to completing the faces. The project was abandoned, and only in 1998 was the vision partially fulfilled when a titanium vault containing enamel panels with important documents was buried inside the unfinished tunnel. The room is not accessible to tourists.

5. Cultural Impact: Shrine or Sacrilege?

Mount Rushmore is the national monument that most bluntly embodies the cultural duality of the United States:

  • The Wound of the Lakota Sioux: The mountain is located in the heart of the "Black Hills" (or in their language: Paha Sapa). For the Native Americans, this is the center of the world and absolutely sacred land. In 1868, the US government signed a treaty guaranteeing the area belonged to the Native Americans forever. However, when gold was discovered in the area a few years later, the government broke the treaty, conquered the territory, and expelled the tribes. For Native Americans, carving the faces of presidents (some with a history of anti-Indian policies) into their sacred mountain is perceived as the ultimate desecration and provocation.
  • The Answer: Crazy Horse Memorial: As a historical counter-response to Mount Rushmore, construction began in 1948 (about 17 miles away) on an even more massive monument carved into rock. It depicts the Lakota hero, Chief "Crazy Horse." When completed, it will be the largest sculpture in the world, aiming to remind the world that "the red man has great heroes of his own."
